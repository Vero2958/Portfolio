Single LIF neuron and LIF network model


When I entered Vogels lab, I knew absolutely nothing at all about modeling. I couldn't even code a single LIF neuron, so obviosuly that's where I started from!

I implemented the mathematics of a simple LIF neuron. The model simulates membrane potential dynamics using math-based RC circuit discretization and features a conditional timer that deterministically manages both absolute and relative refractory periods through dynamic threshold scaling. Nothing fancy here, just a working LIF neuron :D

I then created 40 of them and randomly connected them to create a dense network. For biological plausiblity purposes, I added stochastic noise and randomized refractory periods (between 10 and 20 ms, in the single neurons, it was fixed at 15 ms). I also did a vectorized version of this network using numpy.

Ben-Yishai et al., 1995 (Ring Attractor Model)


Obviously, the first thing Tim did when I arrived in the lab was to give me a shit ton of papers to read (well deserved ahaha). Among these papers, there was one that I found particularly elegant and interesting (and that is still one of my favorites to this day), Ben-Yishai's 1995 Theory of Orientation Tuning in the Visual Cortex.

Now, of course I couldn't properly grasp the math on the spot, but I found the results to be so interesting that I decided to spend a whole week doing the whole derivation by hand (Yep, that ended up being a good ten pages of calculations).

I showed my work to Tim and he quickly caught on to the fact that I really enjoyed that paper, so he told me to implement it in Python, to start doing a bit of serious coding.

Now, Ben-Yishai's 1995 Ring model is a mean-field model. Neurons are organized in hypercolumns, which are characterized by their preferred orientation. The connectivity is also peculiar, each column forms excitatory connections with the columns immediately close to it and with a similar orientation preference, while at the same time creating long-range inhibitory connections (through interneurons) with columns with different orientation selectivities. Such connectivity is called mexican hat connectivity, as it looks like a sombrero hat. Tim told me this specific connectivity was hiding something, and set me off on a hunt to try and figure out what it was they were hiding. My implementation was working perfectly fine, the behaviour was exactly as described in the paper, so I was really puzzled about what he meant with that. My approach was therefore the following: I stared at the screen, stared at the equations, and then back at the screen, in turn for about as long as I needed to figure out that there was nothing wrong with the implementation, and that maybe that was the whole point, so I started to frantically change all the parameters I had available in all possible ways.

I asked myself a gazillion questions: "What happens if I make the excitation stronger?

", "what happens if I make it weaker?", "what happens if I add noise?", "what happens if I load this thing onto a rocket and shoot it into space?" (This last one was probably due to stress ahaha). Eventually, I figured out what Tim was referring to. The paper said nothing about time. There were only 2 dimensions considered: Orientation and Activation Frequency, but no mention of how the bump evolved over time. Turns out that under the paper's parameters, if you give it an input the activity stays on while the stimulus is present and then just goes back to baseline. However, if you modify the ratio of excitation and inhibition such that local excitation becomes stronger and global inhibition becomes weaker, activity can persist after the stimulus is gone thanks to the recurrent excitation generated within the circuit. This, although at first may appear to be a bug (because what do you mean I'm still seeing the stimulus after it is gone?), is actually a feature (see what I've done there? ;D), because it is the mechanism that allows for working memory.

After finally realizing this, and since I had observed very interesting dynamics while I was manipulating the model by adding noise, I decided to look at how the persistent activity would react to somewhat realistic noise conditions. I added 4 types of noise, and the result was surprising: after the input was gone, there was nothing there anymore to keep the activity anchored on the correct orientation, so it would start drifting, exhibiting what I later realized was basically an abstraction of representational drift.

Sussillo et al., 2009 (FORCE Learning)


Generating Coherent Patterns of Activity from Chaotic Neural Networks, by Sussillo and Abbott, is another very interesting paper I reproduced during the first part of my internship.

The paper presents FORCE learning, but what is that exactly? FORCE stands for "first-order reduced and controlled error" and it’s a training procedure which uses only synaptic modifications to control the feedback signal and generate the desired output. It is based on a recursive least-squares (RLS) algorithm, and unlike traditional approaches, which generally perform a sequence of modifications aimed at minimizing initially large errors, in FORCE errors are always small, and the goal is reducing the amount of modifications needed to keep them that way.

The paper applies FORCE to 3 different firing-rate networks with different architectures. The first one and the third are a simple RNN connected to a readout unit through some weights, the difference between the 2 is that in the case of the first network, the weights inside the RNN are fixed and can't be updated by FORCE, so the feedback comes from the readout unit itself, meanwhile in the case of the third, the weights inside the RNN are modified and therefore the feedback from the readout back to the RNN is not needed anymore. The second one on the other hand handles feedback in a completely different way, through a feedback network, which is implemented as a second, smaller RNN connected to the first one through some weights from and to it, and only these weights are updated by FORCE.

I implemented all 3 architectures and used FORCE to train them to produce a sinusoid. In all 3 cases, the activity was chaotic in the beginning, would quickly start to generate the target function after turning on FORCE, and would keep following it after the learning ended. What I found most interesting about this work is not the learning algorithm itself, but rather the fact that networks that were initially chaotic (that is, their scaling parameter g was greater than 1) would train faster, reach lower errors, and find more robust weight solutions than silent networks, and the best performance was found in networks at the edge of chaos (g = 1.56). This seems to suggest that chaos could act as a functional substrate that makes the networks more prone to learning and flexible enough to easily adapt to new dynamics, which could be a good argument for justifying the presence of chaotic activity observed in biological neural networks as well.

Hennequin et al., 2014 (SOC model)


Before starting my own project, I worked on "Optimal control of transient dynamics in balanced networks supports generation of complex movements", which presents a very interesting model called SOC (Stability Optimized Circuit).

While I won't deny that I didn't really enjoy reading it (If your paper sounds more complex than the work actually is, maybe you should review your wording choices ;D), the insights themselves were really interesting, especially considering that balance was emerging autonomously as a consequence of an optimization that wasn't directly constraining the network to be balanced.

SOCs are firing-rate network models that have the peculiarity of being derivable from any random connectivity matrix through an optimization algorithm. In short, the algorithm (based on Lyapunov equations) adjusts inhibitory weights through a gradient descent on the smoothed spectral abscissa, before enforcing 3 connectivity constraints (Dale's law, sparsity and inhibition on average 3 times stronger than excitation) for biological plausibility. The resulting networks are balanced and capable of amplifying any input aligned with their amplifying direction through a spring-loaded box mechanism characterized by a slow ramp and a fast decay of the activity back to baseline. The ramp "loads" the activity by bringing the network into a state aligned with the preferred input direction that, when released, momentarily breaks E/I balance and originates fast, complex and multiphasic transients. These dynamics follow the same progression as the dynamics observed experimentally in the motor cortex, where a preparation period of activity similar to a steady state precedes a transient generated at movement onset.

I implemented both the network and the optimization algorithm, and then, to demonstrate that the dynamics that the network was generating were complex enough to potentially generate movements, I fitted a linear regression on the activity and "trained it" (although we can't really call it that) to decode a few movement trajectories of different shapes from it.

Afterwards, I conducted an exploratory analysis on the network, to figure out how the dynamics were emerging, how noise would influence them, and how each parameter affected them. Out of curiosity, I also tried to apply the optimization algorithm to the excitatory weights as well.

Since the abscissa determines whether the network is stable or not, I tried creating networks with a range of different abscissa values. Turns out that the more stable the network, the faster it returns to baseline, but interestingly these types of networks also produce on average stronger transients. Cross 1 and they become unstable, so unstable that the activity will become chaotic and won't return to baseline after the movement onset. The radius of the eigenspectrum also changes the dynamics, not as drastically as the abscissa, but a radius bigger than 12 ends up creating a substrate for instability and runaway excitation. Also, too much noise and it will completely dilute and eat the dynamics, too much non-linearity and the dynamics will once again converge to chaos. Lastly, as I said I tried optimizing the excitatory weights as well, and it seems like the 2 currents start competing with each other as soon as the dynamics depart from baseline (that is, as soon as we try to load the state that will produce the transients), inhibition tries to match excitation, and excitation tries to match inhibition, therefore they end up following each other and the activity becomes loosely balanced but unstable.
